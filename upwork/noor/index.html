Overview

A media studio sent me an 18-page script: a debate in an AI conference hall, a friendship that follows it, and six comic sketches cutting through it. I turned it into a 17-minute animated episode — every character, every shot, every voice and every cut. It’s the longest AI film I have made, and the one I’m proudest of.

At a glance

The ask

One episode of an AI story series, around fifteen minutes, from an 18-page script.

A speaker-led drama with B-roll made in Veo 3 or Flow, broken by humorous sketches.

The constraint

Every shot is eight seconds long, and the same faces have to hold for seventeen minutes.

Two visual worlds — a cinematic drama and 3D cartoon sketches — in one film.

What I built

A cast and a set locked before production, then the film prompt by prompt.

136 prompt turns became 386 Veo clips, cut on a nine-track timeline.

The outcome

A 17:12 episode, delivered in four parts in under six weeks.

All five milestones approved. The client planned Episode 2 next.

Notes, applied

Every note became
a visible change

The client’s notes were specific, and so were the fixes. Each one below is on screen, before and after, taken from the files themselves.

The venue · style notes
“The storyline takes place in a conference hall, but in Example 1 it looked more like a classroom with a chalkboard.”

The chalkboard lecture room became an AI-conference auditorium — round stage, giant screen, blue house lights — and every scene on it was rebuilt from that one set.

Ashley · style notes
“For this one, we kindly request a simple, natural face design without makeup.”

The test’s blonde, made-up Ashley was redesigned from scratch: wavy dark hair, freckles, a knit sweater — and kept identical across the café, her home and the beach.

The lighting · test round 1
“Professor’s scene’s lighting and Yasmin’s lighting are different, like these are two different classrooms.”

Regraded the Professor’s shots to sit in the same room as Noor’s. It’s why the finished episode is graded to one rule: cold stage blue against warm amber.

The voices · style notes
“Also can we make please the narrator woman.”

The characters speak inside their own Veo shots — the dialogue and lip-sync are written into each prompt. The narrator is ElevenLabs, and moved to a warm female voice for the final cut.

The set

One hall,
every angle

An 8-second generator has no memory of the room it made last time. So the hall was built once as a still, then re-shot from every angle a director would ask for — bird’s-eye, reverse, Dutch tilt, worm’s-eye — and each Veo shot started from one of those plates.

Two worlds

A drama,
and six cartoons inside it

The script breaks its own seriousness six times with a sketch. Each one needed its own cast, its own set and a completely different render style — then had to hand back to the drama without a jolt. A title card opens every sketch and a nasheed carries the switch.

From line to shot

One script line,
one prompt, one shot

Production ran through a single ChatGPT thread — 136 prompt turns from 1 Nov to 9 Dec. I fed it the next line of the script and a direction in my own words; it came back as a shot-level Veo prompt with camera move, lighting, lip-synced dialogue and style. Flow names each clip after the first words of its prompt, so every shot can be traced.

The script lineThe next beat, pasted verbatim, with the dialogue to sync.
My directionFraming, move, mood — often in Arabic, always specific.
The Veo promptCamera, lens, colour temperature, performance, lip-sync, style.
Takes, then the cutGenerated in Flow until it held, then placed on the timeline.

Decisions

Four calls that
made it possible

None of these are on screen. All of them are why seventeen minutes of AI film hold together.

Sized the job before starting

A “15-second” sample came to 27 seconds on screen. Reading the final script, I told the client early that the episode would run well past fifteen minutes once the sketches were in. It finished at 17:12 — and nobody was surprised.

Locked the cast before the shots

Veo forgets a face between clips. So the leads were designed, varied and turned around before a single production shot — and every shot started from the same looks for six weeks.

Built the hall once

Instead of letting each shot invent its own room, I made one conference-hall plate and re-shot it from every angle. Reverse shots match, the stage stays put, and the audience is the same audience.

Gave the sketches their own world

Rather than fight the style jump, I made it a feature: a 3D cartoon world with its own cast, opened every time by the same warm title card and a nasheed — as the script asked.
